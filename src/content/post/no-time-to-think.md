I packed two books for Kefalonia. Eight days, the 16th to the 23rd, a hotel, the sea, no meetings, and the standard holiday delusion that this time the reading would actually happen. I came home having read nothing at all.

Martynka is almost eleven and she wanted something from me from morning until evening, so that is what she got: the pool, the sea, the slides, the next thing invented before the previous one had properly finished. Other parents joined in occasionally. As far as I could tell, I was the only parent joining in from morning until evening, every day, behaving like one of the children. The complete productive output of the week was a few fifteen-minute maths sessions and two Cambly Kids English lessons.

By my usual standards that is a failed week. I want to leave it looking like a failure for a while, because it took me until the following weekend to work out what it actually was.

## The cost of constant motion

The thinking started on Saturday, at home, with a rested head. I sat down and thought about work, time, goals and the shape of the next few years, and the notable part is not the conclusions. It is that the act itself was unusual.

Most weeks I run inside the wheel and congratulate myself on the pace. Work fills the day, then training, logistics, errands, learning and family fill whatever is left, and I can stay disciplined and efficient for months without once stopping long enough to ask whether the direction still makes sense. The largest cost of constant activity is not fatigue. Fatigue is recoverable and reasonably honest about itself. The real cost is that a tired mind cannot evaluate direction, so the question stops being asked, and nothing in the calendar ever flags its absence.

Work irritates me for several ordinary reasons, but the deeper one only became visible once I was rested enough to see it. Work does not simply take hours from the day. It takes the specific hours in which I think best. There are two or three real cognitive windows in a day, periods where the mind is fresh enough to understand a hard problem, make a decision that will still look correct in a year, or build something that did not exist before. I sell almost all of them. I spend them on problems selected by other people, frequently on behalf of people I would never give that much time to if money were solved, and occasionally on behalf of people who actively irritate me. Then in the evening, with whatever attention survived, I am supposed to design my own life.

This is not an argument against employment. That version is intellectually cheap, because every economic exchange is a sale of time. The precise complaint is narrower.

> I sell the hours in which I think best, then try to design my own life with the leftovers.

So the autonomy I want is not the ability to stop working. It is the ability to reserve the best hours for problems I chose deliberately.

## Two thousand weeks

I had the number two thousand in my head as the length of a life, which is wrong. Eighty years is about four thousand one hundred weeks. Two thousand is roughly what I have left: I am forty-six, and if I reach eighty-five that is around two thousand weeks, give or take a bad decade.

The number is not tragic and it is not a coaching prop. It is arithmetic behaving impolitely. Two thousand sounds generous until it gets converted into summers while my daughter still wants to spend them with me, years of good health, and the limited remaining number of times I will have the energy to start something difficult from zero.

Time does not only pass while I rest or waste it. It also passes while I am being useful, and that is the dangerous version, because it looks respectable and produces receipts. A person can spend three decades working hard, meeting obligations, improving systems and delivering other people's targets without ever seriously deciding what his own targets are. Brian Tracy put it bluntly: if you do not have goals of your own, you end up working on somebody else's. I had heard the same thing from Jim Rohn and half a dozen others, and I agreed with it for years. Agreement is cheap. I had simply never given the selection of a goal anything close to the seriousness I later gave its execution.

## A disciplined soldier with a careless commander

The pattern is easy to see now. I would settle on a target quickly, sometimes in an afternoon, and then work on it like a professional for three months. Once the direction was fixed, discipline was rarely the constraint. I could build the plan, track it, tolerate the repetition and keep going after the motivation had gone. What I never did properly was choose.

The asymmetry is absurd when written down. Hundreds of hours of execution, defended by twenty minutes of selection. I would not accept that ratio in any system I design at work, where nobody gets to skip the requirements phase because they are enthusiastic about implementation.

So I finally ran Rohn's framework properly instead of nodding at it:

- write out around fifty things I might want to do, have, become, experience or give,
- assign each one a horizon of one, three, five or ten years,
- check the spread, because a list where forty items are professional is a diagnosis rather than a plan,
- group, cut, and select four goals per horizon,
- write the why for each one, individually, in words.

It took seven hours. That sounds excessive for a list and negligible for something meant to point the next ten years. For the first time the result did not feel like another project I had found. It felt like a direction I had chosen.

## Look where you are going

On the terrace in Kefalonia I watched a child of about seven carrying a cup of tea to a table. The kid stared at the cup the entire way, which is the natural thing to do and precisely the wrong one. Watching the cup does not steady the cup. It turns every step into a correction and makes the spill more likely, not less.

Metaphors have technical limits and I am not suggesting anyone walk around with their eyes fixed on the horizon, which is how you find the stairs. The narrow version is enough: control what you are carrying, but look at where it is going. Most of my calendar is the child staring at the cup. The tasks are completed, the email is answered, the session is logged, the problem directly in front of me stays under control. Control is not direction.

## Recognition, not discovery

After I got home I listened to Dry Creek Dewayne, an old rancher who has ended up as an influencer, which is a sentence I did not expect to write and does not change the fact that he is worth listening to.

I noticed the same mechanism that drew me deeper into Jim Rohn. Both of them stated things I had already begun to reach on my own. Recognition created trust, and trust made me curious about where the same road went next. It works like vocabulary: I can study a word several times and lose it, then hear it used once in a real conversation and keep it permanently. The external voice does not create the thought. It fixes it in place.

Dewayne made two points that did exactly that. The first was the one I had reached that same weekend: when a person works hard and then stays productive after work, there is never a moment quiet enough to think. Productivity is an excellent hiding place from strategy, and it is respected, which makes it a better hiding place than laziness.

The second was sharper. He asked what difference it makes to the people around you whether you lift 180 pounds or 200. Usually none. What it changes is your opinion of yourself.

## What the training was for

I care about progress and I do not intend to stop. I want to run faster, get stronger, improve the 5K, and see evidence that the work is still producing adaptation rather than managed decline. Numbers matter because they make feedback difficult to fake. But measurable is not the same as meaningful, and the measurable thing is always the easier one to remember.

Would five seconds off a 5K materially improve my daughter's life? No. Would another five kilograms on a lift make me a better father? Almost certainly not. What does matter is being aerobically fit enough to spend the week in the water, on the slides, up and down stairs, in constant motion, behaving like a ten-year-old alongside an almost eleven-year-old, without sitting at the edge of the pool because my back, my knees or my conditioning had made playing too expensive.

That was the actual return on years of training, collected in Kefalonia, and it appears on no watch. Not a race time. Capacity. A body that let me participate instead of supervise.

None of this means personal records are worthless, and I am not proposing a rule that every private ambition has to justify itself through service to somebody else. That converts life into one more obligation, and enjoying something is a legitimate reason to pursue it. But it is a useful filter for the specific moment when ego starts presenting itself as purpose:

> If nobody would ever know the result, would it still improve the life I am building?

Sometimes the answer is still yes. Sometimes the question ends the argument.

## The part I have not solved

The books came home unread, which was the correct outcome. I only know that because a week away bought me two clear mornings in which to work it out.

I have a direction now, and I have a system for reviewing it. What I do not yet have is a reliable place for this kind of thinking inside an ordinary week. Deciding direction is not the reward for finishing the important work. It is part of the important work, and it is still the easiest part to leave unscheduled.

I have a map now. What I still do not have is a reliable place in an ordinary week to question it.
